Chapter 20 Written lab report guidelines

Skills this lab builds. 4DEE: Ecology Practices → Communicating and applying ecology. BioSkills: Communication & Collaboration → “Share ideas, data, and findings with others clearly and accurately”; “Use appropriate language and style to communicate science effectively to targeted audiences”; Process of Science → “Make evidence-based arguments using your own and others’ findings.”

This chapter is the reference several of the labs point back to. Read it before you write your first lab report, and come back to the rubric at the end of this chapter before you submit — that rubric is what your TA grades with.

A well-written scientific report must:

  • Be clear, concise, and organized
  • Completely describe the procedures that were followed and the results that were obtained
  • Relate results to the existing state of knowledge (using citations) and interpret their significance — what do the results mean?

20.1 General rules

Lab reports should be formatted as follows:

  • Typed
  • Double spaced
  • 12 point font
  • 1 inch margins
  • Page numbers
  • Proof-read!
  • Label sections (Introduction, Methods, Results, etc.)

20.1.1 Needed components

  • Title page — your name, class and section, date, and the abstract. This should be on its own separate page.
  • Body of the text:
    • Full introduction, addressing all five questions outlined in the specific instructions below, including references to published literature on the topic
    • Methods & Analysis
    • Results
    • Discussion & Conclusion
  • Tables & Figures — on a separate page
  • Literature Cited — must reference a minimum of 3 peer-reviewed scientific journals

20.2 Components and instructions

20.2.1 Abstract (write last)

A brief summary of the purpose of your experiment, the hypotheses you tested, how you performed your test, what results were found, what hypotheses were supported or rejected, and what your results imply with regard to the research question. The abstract will probably be about 5–6 sentences in length.

20.2.2 Introduction (write after results)

This section should explain the objectives of the study, and why it is a worthwhile effort. Be as specific as possible. Please be sure to address the following questions:

  • Why did you undertake this study?
  • What is the existing state of knowledge about this topic? Refer to outside sources to answer this question and use in-text citations.
  • What are your research questions and objectives?
  • What is your hypothesis? Include a “because” statement.
  • What result(s) would you expect to see if your hypothesis is supported?

Previous studies are used often to help focus your audience toward the specific questions and the scope of the current study. If there are readings associated with this lab, you should refer to those papers here, as well as in the discussion. You will most likely need to find a few outside sources as well.

20.2.3 Methods & Analysis (write first)

Step-by-step instructions on how the lab was performed, in sentence form, using your own words. This section must answer all the basic questions about the way the study was conducted: what, when, where, and how.

WHAT. What are the organism(s) being studied? Explain why this is an appropriate unit of study for the research. Italicize all scientific names — e.g. Mountain goat (Oreamnos americanus). Be certain to reference your sources if plant or animal descriptions are taken from the literature.

WHEN. Give the time periods during which the work was done — year, month, day, time of day — whenever this is relevant to the type of data collected. Describe the special conditions of the organism (developmental or reproductive state) in the context of the seasonal cycle, or within other natural cycles such as weather, that prevailed during the study period.

WHERE. Location — latitude and longitude, elevation, site, state.

HOW. Explain the techniques of collecting data or conducting experiments, and describe the equipment used. Where these procedures and equipment are standard, do this by reference to other publications. Otherwise, describe them in enough detail that a reader can duplicate them. State the techniques used to record, summarize, and analyze the data.

Although you should try to cover all four topics in your methods section, it is neither necessary nor advisable to break them apart into subheadings. The best written methods cover materials and methods with well written paragraphs.

20.2.4 Results (write second)

Include all data and statistics. Do not include raw, un-summarized data in this section, but referring to specific numbers that illustrate trends or significant data points is encouraged. Actual data sheets, if included in the lab report, should be in an appendix. Data should be summarized and analyzed — means, standard deviation, and so on — and presented as visually as possible. Well constructed graphs are clearer than tables for most data.

Refer to the figures and tables within a narrative, describing the trends. In other words, walk the reader through your results. The opening paragraph should state the overall trends found in the data — but do not say SIGNIFICANT difference unless you did the statistics to test for significance. The following paragraphs should give the specific findings that support the overall trend.

Do not say “See tables for results” or “Figure one shows that…” or “(See Figure 1).” Say:

There were more herbivores than primary producers (Fig. 1).

This section should contain, in summarized form, the data and observations obtained in the study. Use complete sentences and paragraph formatting. Refer to each table and figure individually in this section. In general, the presentation of data as a table or figure is sufficient; it is usually not necessary that the same data be presented in both ways. To be complete and concise in the text, concentrate on general patterns, trends and differences in the results and not on the details of the numbers themselves — although referring to averages, or even individual numbers that illustrate a point, is very useful.

For labs with statistical analyses, you should make every attempt to combine statements about the significance of differences with a precise indication, in parentheses, of the test used and the probability. For example:

The difference between means of two samples was highly significant (paired t test; t = 6.35, DF = 11, p < 0.01; Sokal and Rohlf 1981).

Use the metric system and other international units whenever possible. Write numbers as numerals whenever they are associated with measurement units (e.g. 3 meters) or are parts of dates or mathematical expressions. In other cases, spell them out for numbers less than 10 (e.g. five cells), and give them as numerals for larger values (e.g. 21 cells).

The results section should summarize the data but should be free of interpretation of the data. Any explanation, interpretation, or “because” statements should not appear in the results, but in the discussion. This is frequently difficult, because it is very tempting when first presenting data to tell why the results turned out as they did, or what the results mean. Force yourself to maintain the critical distinction between results and their interpretations.

20.2.5 Discussion & Conclusion (write after results)

This is the appropriate section to interpret the data in relation to the original objectives or hypotheses, and to relate these interpretations to the state of knowledge and future needs for research. A common mistake is to repeat the results at some higher level of generality; the discussion section should be genuinely interpretive.

Your discussion should start with an interpretation of your results and then expand to explain your results in the context of relevant published work. This can be achieved by following the outline below — feel free to modify the order of these points to achieve a logical flow in your writing:

  • Reach conclusions about the initial question(s) or hypothesis(es).
  • Identify sources of error and basic inadequacies of technique, and suggest improvements in methods or approaches, if any.
  • Raise questions and discuss ambiguous data.
  • State how your results relate to or build upon previous studies, and compare your conclusions to those reached by other related studies.
  • Suggest future studies that should be carried out to further knowledge on this topic. Mention practical applications for the research.
  • Speculate upon broader meanings of the conclusions reached.

You should end this section with 1–2 sentences that restate your main conclusions, speak to their importance, and note how they relate back to your research question.

20.2.6 Tables & Figures

Technically part of results, but goes at the end of the body of the report as its own section.

  • Located after discussion and conclusion
  • Referenced in text. Example: Our results showed that as pinecone height increases, pinecone weight also increases (Figure 1).
  • Labeled with title, axis labels including units, and a legend if applicable
  • Contains a caption, numbered (Figure 1, Table 1, etc.). This gives context to the data — a miniature summary of the project as it pertains to the data presented — an interpretation of the graph, and a discussion of the implications of the project results. Use complete sentences and scientific language.

20.2.7 Literature Cited

This section should contain, in alphabetical order, only those items specifically referred to in the text. For your lab reports, please follow the formats below, which provide all the essential information.

Journal article

Shiels, A.B. and R.L. Sanford, Jr. 2001. Soil nutrient differences between two krummholz-form tree species and adjacent alpine tundra. Geoderma 102:205–217.

This reference includes the names of the authors, the year of publication, the article title, the fully spelled journal name, and the volume and page numbers of the article itself.

Book

Molles, Jr. M.C. 2004. Ecology: Concepts and Applications (3rd Edition). McGraw Hill, Boston, MA, USA. 586 pages.

This reference gives the author, date of publication, title, and the publisher’s name and location.

Article in an edited volume

Sanford, Jr., R.L. and E. Cuevas. 1996. Root growth and rhizosphere interactions in tropical forests. Pages 268–300 in S.S. Mulkey, R.L. Chazdon, and A.P. Smith (eds.) Tropical Forest Plant Ecophysiology. Chapman and Hall, New York, NY, USA.

This reference includes the article authors, the date of publication, the article title, pages in volume, names of volume editors, volume title, and the publisher’s name and location.

Proper in-text citation format

Paraphrase references and cite them in the Literature Cited section. Do not use quotations or footnotes. Here are three examples of proper citation format:

  • Shiels and Sanford (2001) found that…
  • Soil nutrients above tree lines show patterns that are… (Shiels and Sanford 2001).
  • Phosphorus fractions in the surface soils (Shiels and Sanford 2001) are correlated with…

If there are more than two authors, it is common to abbreviate with (Sanford et al. 1985). The abbreviation et al. is for the Latin phrase meaning “and others.”

This is what your TAs are using to grade your lab report.

Table 20.1: Lab report rubric. Total: 70 points.
Section Points Criteria
Style 10 Uses acceptable style, grammar and spelling throughout the paper; Meticulously proofread; Follows assigned format, and all in the same font
Cover Page and Abstract 5 Includes scientific title, name, lab section and date (see published scientific papers for title examples); Abstract summarizes the main points of your paper; One paragraph in length; Includes brief background and significance of the study (1-2 sentences); Describes methods and hypothesis tested (1-2 sentences); Describes major results - was your hypothesis supported or refuted? (1 sentence)
Introduction 10 Provides a clear, concise and logical introduction and background to your research study; Discusses previous knowledge about the topic being studied, referencing outside sources; Describes your evidence (if/then statements, as they relate to the hypothesis); Outside sources included and cited properly but not quoted directly - long quotes and copy/pasting will lose points; Ends with a clear statement of the research questions and hypotheses to be addressed
Methods & Analysis 10 Describes the procedure followed concisely, in past tense; May reference photos to better display project design (put the photos in the tables and figures section); Includes any deviations from the regular procedure
Results 10 A minimum of 2 figures and 2 relevant tables, referenced in the text; All significant data also described in words, with important numbers and values specifically referenced, such as p-values or outliers; Includes statistical analysis if that was part of the lab; No interpretation of the data here - just report the results
Discussion/Conclusion 15 Results are interpreted in the context of the hypothesis and research question posed in the introduction - was it significant? was there correlation?; Comments on possible relationships between variables; Possible sources of error or limitations are explained; Considers how this might apply to similar problems, other research, the broader context, and future investigations; Extrapolates on the data and makes predictions about what might be observable in similar environments
Tables and Figures 5 Figures and tables display appropriate information given your hypothesis; Figures and tables have proper labels, captions and legends, all of which should be readable; May include photographs of your results
References 5 Minimum of 3 peer-reviewed scientific journals in the references; Cited correctly in the text and in the references section
